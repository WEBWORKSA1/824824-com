Eight hours of work, eight of rest and eight for yourself: it sounds like common sense now, but it took more than a century of organizing, strikes and lawmaking to make it normal. Here is how the 24-hour day got divided into thirds, and where the idea is heading next.

Robert Owen and the eight-hour idea

When the Industrial Revolution filled Britain's mills, workdays far longer than eight hours were normal, for adults and children alike. One of the first people to push back from the inside was Robert Owen, a mill owner and social reformer at New Lanark in Scotland.

According to the National Museum of Australia, Owen first proposed the eight-hour day in 1817 at New Lanark. The idea is usually summed up in a slogan attributed to him that divides the day into thirds: "Eight hours labour, eight hours recreation, eight hours rest." That simple formula became the rallying cry of eight-hour campaigns far beyond Britain.

Melbourne, 1856: the stonemasons win

Some of the first workers to turn the slogan into reality were building tradesmen in Melbourne, Australia. On 21 April 1856, stonemasons working on a new building at the University of Melbourne stopped work and marched to Parliament House, picking up masons from other sites along the way.

Employers agreed to talks, and the masons won an eight-hour day at the wage they had previously earned for 10 hours. Early on, the gain reached mainly the building trades rather than the workforce as a whole. According to the museum, eight-hour legislation came to Victoria and New South Wales only in 1916, and a 40-hour, five-day week was approved for all Australians in 1948.

The fight in America: 1866 to Haymarket

1866: the first national call

After the Civil War, American unions came together in a national federation. The National Labor Union was founded on August 20, 1866, in Baltimore. Its resolutions called on Congress to make eight hours the legal workday, the first national demand of its kind. The call went unheeded at the time, and the union folded in 1873, but the idea was now part of the national debate.

1886: May Day and Haymarket

Unions then set May 1, 1886, as the start of a nationwide general strike for the eight-hour day. In Chicago, tens of thousands of workers walked off the job. On May 3, police shot and killed strikers outside the McCormick reaper works.

The next evening, police moved in to break up a protest meeting near Haymarket Square, and someone threw a bomb into their ranks. Police opened fire, and both officers and civilians died in the bombing and the shooting that followed.

Eight men were convicted. Four were hanged on November 11, 1887, and a fifth took his own life the day before the executions. In 1893, Illinois Governor John Peter Altgeld pardoned the three surviving defendants and declared their trial unfair.

The strike date outlived the tragedy. Within four years, May 1 was being marked as International Workers' Day, according to the Library of Congress, and many countries still observe it.

Ford's eight-hour shifts and the $5 day

Industry didn't adopt eight-hour shifts just because labor asked; employers had to see a payoff. On January 5, 1914, Ford Motor Company announced it would cut the workday at its Highland Park plant from nine hours to eight while roughly doubling earnings with a $5 day structured as profit-sharing.

The shorter day let Ford add a third production shift, so three eight-hour shifts could keep the plant running around the clock: the "8 of 24" model in its purest industrial form. About 10,000 job seekers showed up at the plant the next day. Ford says the policy helped cut daily absenteeism from 10% to under 1% by 1916.

1919: the eight-hour day goes international

After World War I, the Treaty of Versailles created the International Labour Organization. Its constitution singled out an eight-hour day or 48-hour week as an urgent priority. The first convention the ILO adopted, at its first conference in Washington in 1919, was the Hours of Work (Industry) Convention, known as Convention No. 1.

Its core rule capped working hours in industry at eight a day and 48 a week, with limited exceptions. The convention entered into force on June 13, 1921. Relatively few countries formally ratified it, but the ILO credits it with helping make the eight-hour day standard practice worldwide.

The US Fair Labor Standards Act and the 40-hour week

In the United States, the eight-hour day eventually met the five-day week. President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the Fair Labor Standards Act on June 25, 1938, and it took effect on October 24, 1938. The law covered industries employing about one-fifth of the labor force and set a 25-cent minimum wage for its first year.

Rather than impose 40 hours at once, the law phased the limit in:

PeriodMaximum workweek before overtime
First year (from October 24, 1938)44 hours
Second year42 hours
From October 24, 194040 hours

Hours beyond the limit had to be paid at time and a half. Notice how the law worked: it set a weekly threshold, not a daily one, and made hours beyond it more expensive. Federal overtime still counts hours by the workweek today, as our overtime laws guide explains.

Example: the math behind 40

Under the 1919 ILO standard, eight hours a day and 48 a week meant a six-day week. Keep the eight-hour day but cap the week at 40 hours, and you get five working days and two days off: 5 × 8 = 40.

Today: four-day week trials

The newest chapter asks whether 40 hours is still the right number. In a high-profile pilot, 61 UK companies with around 2,900 workers tried a four-day week from June to December 2022. Firms had to keep pay at 100% while giving staff a meaningful cut in working time. Companies were recruited by 4 Day Week Global, the 4 Day Week Campaign and the research organization Autonomy, and the results were analyzed by researchers including teams at Boston College and the University of Cambridge.

The results, published in February 2023, found that:

  • 56 of the 61 companies (92%) were continuing with the four-day week, and 18 said the change was permanent.
  • 71% of employees reported lower levels of burnout at the end of the trial.
  • Revenue was roughly flat across the six months, up 1.4% on average.
  • Staff departures fell by 57% during the trial.
Tip: Read these results with care. The companies chose to take part, so they may be better suited to a shorter week than the average employer, and a six-month trial can't show long-term effects.

Why this site is called 824824

Every schedule on this site, from a 9-to-5 to a 2-2-3 rotation, is an answer to the same old question of how to divide 24 hours. The eight-hour formula gave eight of them to work. That's where our name comes from: 824824 reads as "8 of 24," twice over, a nod to the eight-hour shift and to the round-the-clock workplaces where it still matters most.

Today's shift workers might work 8, 10, 12 or 24 hours at a stretch, but the principle behind the slogan still holds: work, rest and a life of your own all need their share of the day. To see how your own week adds up, try the hours calculator.
