Scheduling a 24/7 operation is a math problem, a health problem and a fairness problem at the same time. Get the math wrong and you are short every night. Get the fairness wrong and your best people leave. This guide walks through coverage math, rotation direction, minimum rest rules and US fair workweek laws, then turns them into practices and a checklist you can use this week.

Important: This guide is general information, not legal advice. Scheduling laws vary by country, state, province and city, and they change, so confirm your obligations with the relevant labor authority or an employment lawyer before you set policy.

Coverage math for 24/7 operations

A week has 168 hours (24 × 7). Every post you keep staffed around the clock needs 168 hours of work a week, whatever the shift length. The basic formula is:

People per post = 168 ÷ average weekly hours per person

Shift lengthShifts per post per weekWith 4 crewsWith 5 crews
8 hours21 (3 a day)42 hours each, about 5.25 shifts a week33.6 hours each, 4.2 shifts a week
12 hours14 (2 a day)42 hours each, 3.5 shifts a week33.6 hours each, 2.8 shifts a week

Shift length does not change the total hours. It changes how often people come in and how many handovers you run each day: three with 8-hour shifts, two with 12s. With four crews, each person averages 42 hours a week, so wherever overtime starts at 40 hours, some weeks will include overtime. Check the overtime laws guide before you lock in a pattern.

Example: staffing a unit that needs 3 people on duty

Three posts × 168 hours = 504 staffed hours a week. With staff on 36-hour weeks (three 12-hour shifts), you need 504 ÷ 36 = 14 people. Then add relief for vacation, sickness and training. Say your own records show people are unavailable 10% of scheduled time: 14 ÷ 0.90 = 15.6, so plan for 16. Use your real absence data, not a rule of thumb.

Forward vs backward rotation

If people rotate between shifts, the direction matters:

  • Forward (clockwise) rotation moves days → evenings → nights, so each new shift starts later.
  • Backward (counterclockwise) rotation moves nights → evenings → days, so each new shift starts earlier.

NIOSH training material for nurses notes that forward rotations are easier to adjust to than backward ones (NIOSH, CDC). The same page cites researchers who recommend at least 11 hours between two shifts. Backward rotation without days off in between makes that hard. Someone who finishes an evening shift at 11 p.m. and starts a day shift at 7 a.m. gets only 8 hours off, and that has to cover the commute, meals and sleep.

Minimum rest between shifts

Rest rules are the hard limits your schedule must respect:

European Union11 consecutive hours in every 24-hour periodAverage week, including overtime, may not exceed 48 hours

Canada (federally regulated)8 consecutive hours between shiftsWritten schedule at least 96 hours ahead; written notice at least 24 hours before a shift change or addition

US fair workweek and predictive scheduling laws

A growing set of US laws requires covered employers to post schedules in advance and pay a premium for late changes. Coverage turns on industry, employer size and sometimes worker pay, so read the official page for each location you operate in.

LawWho is coveredSchedule noticeRest between shifts

Most of these laws also share three features:

  • Predictability pay for late employer changes, such as one extra hour of pay when time is added or a shift is moved.
  • A good faith estimate of expected hours, given at hire.
  • A right to decline hours added after the schedule is posted.

This table is not a complete list, and new local laws appear from time to time. Check with the labor office in every city and state where you schedule staff.

Fairness practices that build trust

Legal compliance is the floor. These practices are what make a roster feel fair:

  • Rotate the unpopular shifts. Track each person's nights, weekends and holidays over a rolling quarter. Assign the next undesirable shift to whoever has worked the fewest, and share the counts with the team.
  • Publish the rules. Write down how shifts, holidays and vacation are assigned, including any role seniority plays, so nobody has to guess.
  • Post early and change rarely. Fourteen days of notice is standard under several US laws and makes sense even where it is not required. When something has to change, ask for volunteers before assigning.
  • Give people some control. Preference forms or a self-scheduling window, within coverage limits, cut down on swap requests later.
  • Protect recovery time. Keep runs of consecutive nights short, follow them with full days off, and never schedule a quick return just to fill a gap.

Shift swap rules that work

Swaps keep a schedule flexible, but loose swap rules can quietly break rest rules or create overtime. Put these in writing:

  1. Both people must be qualified for the post they are taking.
  2. The swap cannot break minimum rest or maximum hours, or push anyone into unapproved overtime.
  3. Set a request deadline and a response time for approval.
  4. Record every approved swap in the schedule system, with who agreed and when. Some fair workweek laws require consent and records for changes, and the record protects everyone.
  5. Track who swaps out of weekends or nights, so swapping does not quietly undo your rotation.

Tooling and a pre-publish checklist

A spreadsheet can run a small team, but once you have several crews or locations, look for a scheduling tool that can:

  • show coverage by hour against your minimum staffing;
  • flag rest gaps, maximum hours and notice windows automatically;
  • keep fairness counters for nights, weekends and holidays;
  • run swaps with rule checks and an audit trail;
  • handle daylight saving time changes correctly.

Before you publish any schedule, check:

  1. Every post is covered for all 168 hours, with relief for planned leave.
  2. Every gap between shifts meets the strictest rest rule that applies.
  3. Rotations run forward, and night runs are short and followed by days off.
  4. Undesirable shifts follow your rotation counters.
  5. The schedule goes out with the notice your jurisdiction requires, or 14 days if none applies.
  6. Expected overtime is approved and budgeted.
