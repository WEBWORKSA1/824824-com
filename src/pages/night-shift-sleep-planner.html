How do you like to sleep between shifts?

Sleep between shifts0:00

      The plan assumes you work the same shift the next day. On your first and last nights, see the tips below.

      The hours between two night shifts go fast once you count the drive home, winding down and getting ready again. This planner lays out one full day, from the start of your shift to the start of the next, so you can see how much sleep really fits and when to time light, caffeine and your main meal.

      How to use the night shift sleep planner

      1. Enter your shift. Set Shift starts and Shift ends. Overnight shifts work as entered, so 19:00 to 07:00 is a 12-hour shift.
      2. Add your numbers. Choose Sleep you need (hours), from 6 to 9, then enter your Commute each way (minutes) and Wind-down before sleep (minutes).
      3. Pick a caffeine cut-off. Choose 4, 5, 6 or 8 hours under Caffeine cut-off before sleep (hours).
      4. Choose how you sleep. Pick All at once, Split (an anchor sleep plus a nap before work) or Later (sleep that ends close to your next shift).
      5. Read, print and share. Check the timeline and the timed checklist. Untick Show 24-hour times if you prefer a.m. and p.m., then use Print my plan or Copy share link.

      How the plan is built

      The plan covers 24 hours from the start of your shift and assumes you work the same shift the next day. After the shift it adds your commute and wind-down. Before the next shift it holds back 60 minutes to get ready, plus your commute in. The time left over is your sleep window, and each strategy uses it differently:

      • All at once: one main sleep that starts as soon as you've wound down.
      • Split: an anchor sleep of up to 4.5 hours after work, then the rest of your sleep as a nap that ends when it's time to get ready. The nap is left out if less than 30 minutes would fit.
      • Later: one main sleep that ends when it's time to get ready.

      The caffeine cut-off counts back from the start of your first sleep. Caffeine's half-life averages about 5 hours in healthy adults but ranges from roughly 1.5 to 9.5 hours, according to an Institute of Medicine report, so pick 8 hours if coffee tends to keep you awake.

      For light, the checklist follows the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH): bright light in the first half of your shift and less in the second half where that's safe. It also says to avoid bright morning light on the way home. Sunglasses help with that, but NIOSH cautions they block light's alerting effect, so wear them only if someone else is driving. The checklist also reminds you to eat your main meal before the shift, and the planner warns you when the sleep you asked for doesn't fit.

      Example: a 7 p.m. to 7 a.m. shift

      You need 7 hours of sleep, with a 30-minute commute, 30 minutes to wind down and a 6-hour caffeine cut-off. You're home at 7:30 a.m. and asleep by 8 a.m. To leave at 6:30 p.m. after an hour to get ready, you must be up by 5:30 p.m., which gives a 9.5-hour window. All at once puts sleep from 8 a.m. to 3 p.m., with last caffeine at 2 a.m. and bright light until 1 a.m. Split gives an anchor sleep from 8 a.m. to 12:30 p.m. and a 2.5-hour nap from 3 to 5:30 p.m.

      Your first and last night of a run

      The planner assumes you're mid-run, so plan the edges separately. NIOSH warns against staying awake all day before your first night shift and then working all night, and against staying up the whole day after your last night so you can sleep at night. Either way you're awake for too many hours, which raises your risk of accidents and injuries.

      Before the first night, get sleep where you can, including a nap. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM) notes that even 20 to 30 minutes before a night shift can improve alertness. After the last night, plan some sleep that morning rather than pushing through to bedtime. The shift pattern generator shows where each run of nights starts and ends.

      Keep an anchor sleep

      Anchor sleep is a block of sleep you keep at the same hours every day, on workdays and days off, so your body clock gets some consistency. NIOSH describes a compromise schedule for full-time night workers built around one.

      Example: an anchor from 8 a.m. to noon

      On workdays you sleep from 8 a.m., soon after you get home, until 3 p.m. On days off you stay up until about 4 a.m. and sleep until noon. Both schedules include 8 a.m. to noon, so those four hours are sleep time every day of the week.

      Watch for drowsy driving

      The drive home is part of the shift. NIOSH notes that most drowsy-driving crashes and near misses happen between 4 and 6 a.m. Its warning signs include:

      • Trouble focusing, frequent blinking or heavy eyelids
      • Daydreaming or wandering, disconnected thoughts
      • Not remembering the last few miles, or missing exits and traffic signs
      • Yawning repeatedly or rubbing your eyes
      • Trouble keeping your head up
      • Drifting from your lane, tailgating or hitting a rumble strip
      • Feeling restless and irritable

      If you notice any of them, stop driving as soon as it's safe. Then line up another way home: the AASM suggests a ride or a bus or cab after a night shift, and the Sleep Foundation suggests a brief nap at work or in your car before you leave. Our night shift survival guide goes deeper on sleep, meals and safety.

      Talk to a clinician if you often can't sleep, feel very sleepy at work or have nodded off at the wheel. The AASM lists excessive sleepiness, trouble falling or staying asleep, low energy and poor concentration among the signs of shift work disorder, and the Sleep Foundation suggests seeing a doctor if problems last two weeks or longer.
