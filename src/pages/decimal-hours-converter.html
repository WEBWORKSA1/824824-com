How to use the decimal hours converter

  1. Under Hours and minutes to decimal, enter Hours and Minutes. You get decimal hours rounded to two places, a more precise figure and the total in minutes.
  2. Under Decimal to hours and minutes, type a number from your pay stub or timesheet, such as 8.33, into Decimal hours to see it as hours and minutes.
  3. To convert a whole timesheet, paste one entry per line into Convert and total a list. Entries can look like 7:45, 8h 20m or 450m.
  4. Read each converted line under In decimal hours, with the total for the list underneath. A question mark means a line couldn't be read, so fix it before you use the total.
  5. For a quick lookup, scroll the Minutes to decimal hours chart. It lists every minute from 0 to 59 at two and four decimal places.

How decimal hours work

An hour has 60 minutes, not 100, so to turn minutes into part of an hour you divide by 60. Thirty minutes is half an hour, or 0.50, and 45 minutes is three quarters, or 0.75. That's why 7:45 on a clock becomes 7.75 on a timesheet.

To go back, multiply the decimal part by 60. A pay stub showing 38.40 hours means 38 hours 24 minutes, because 0.40 × 60 = 24. The converter rounds to the nearest whole minute, so 8.33 hours becomes 8:20, since 0.33 × 60 = 19.8.

Any multiple of 3 minutes converts exactly to two decimal places: 6 minutes is 0.10 and 12 minutes is 0.20. Every other value repeats forever and gets rounded, so 20 minutes, which is 0.3333…, shows as 0.33. Those small roundings add up, which is why the list converter adds the minutes first and converts the total once.

Example: totaling a week

Your shifts are 7:45, 8:20, 6:50 and 12:15. Converted one at a time and rounded, they're 7.75, 8.33, 6.83 and 12.25, which add up to 35.16. Add the minutes instead: 465 + 500 + 410 + 735 = 2,110 minutes, or 35 hours 10 minutes. Divided by 60, that's 35.17 hours, which is what the list converter shows.

Rounding, overtime and pay records

Some employers, especially those using time clocks, record start and stop times to the nearest 5 minutes, tenth of an hour or quarter hour. Under 29 CFR 785.48, that's accepted only if, over time, it doesn't leave you unpaid for time you actually worked. If your time card is kept in tenths, each 0.1 is exactly 6 minutes.

Your weekly total also decides overtime. Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), covered workers who aren't exempt must get at least time and a half for hours over 40 in a workweek, as DOL Fact Sheet #23 explains. Employers must also record the hours you work each day and each workweek, according to DOL Fact Sheet #21, so keep your own log to compare. The overtime calculator works out the pay.

Example: a week with overtime

Your week comes to 41 hours 15 minutes. Since 15 ÷ 60 = 0.25, that's 41.25 hours, and 1.25 of them are over 40. At a regular rate of $20 an hour, time and a half is $30, so those 1.25 hours are worth at least 1.25 × $30 = $37.50.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Reading 7.30 as 7 hours 30 minutes. 7.30 decimal hours is 7 hours 18 minutes. Seven and a half hours is 7.50.
  • Typing clock times into a decimal field. Enter 6:50 and 7:40 as 6.50 + 7.40 and you get 13.90, but the real total is 14 hours 30 minutes, or 14.50.
  • Subtracting a break as 0.30. A 30-minute unpaid meal is 0.50 hours, so an 8-hour span with that break is 7.50 hours, not 7.70.
  • Rounding every shift before adding. Add the minutes for the whole week, then convert once.
  • Skipping the overnight math. Work out the length of a shift that crosses midnight with the hours calculator first, then convert it.
  • Accepting one-way rounding. Rounding to the nearest increment goes down as well as up. If it always seems to cost you time, our guide to decimal hours and time card math explains what to check.
