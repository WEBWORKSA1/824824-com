Shift Differential Pay: Rates, Overtime Math and Pay Stub Checks

Updated October 8, 20267 min readBy the 824824 editorial team

On this page

  2. Flat vs percentage differentials
  3. Who typically gets them
  4. How differentials affect US overtime
  5. More worked examples

A shift differential is extra pay for working the hours most people would rather avoid: evenings, nights, weekends or holidays. Over a year it can add up to real money, but only if it is calculated correctly, including in your overtime rate. This guide explains how differentials are structured, how US overtime law treats them, and how to check your pay stub.

Important: This is general information, not legal advice. Pay rules depend on your country, state, employer policy and any union contract, so confirm the details with your payroll team, your union or your jurisdiction's labor authority.

What a shift differential is

A shift differential, sometimes called a shift premium, is an amount added to your base rate for hours worked inside a defined window, such as 11 p.m. to 7 a.m. Employers use differentials to fill unpopular hours and to recognize the strain of working against your body clock.

In most places, no law requires one:

  • United States: The FLSA does not require extra pay for night work. The Department of Labor calls it a matter of agreement between employer and employee, although some public employers are bound by statute, as you will see below.
  • United Kingdom: The National Minimum Wage applies to night workers, but GOV.UK confirms there is no higher legal night rate. Any premium comes from your contract.
  • Australia: Penalty rates for late-night or early-morning shifts, weekends, public holidays and overtime can apply, depending on the award or registered agreement that covers you, according to the Fair Work Ombudsman.

So the first question is always where your differential is written down. Look in your offer letter, employee handbook, union contract or award.

Flat vs percentage differentials

Differentials come in two main forms:

  • Flat amount: a fixed sum per qualifying hour, such as $2.00 extra.
  • Percentage: a share of your base rate, such as 10% extra.

A percentage grows automatically with every raise, while a flat amount stays put until someone renegotiates it. To find the base rate where the two pay the same, divide the flat amount by the percentage: $2.00 ÷ 0.10 = $20.00 an hour.

Check which hours qualify, too. Some policies pay only for hours inside the window, while others pay the whole shift once enough of it falls inside. VA nurses are a good illustration: under 38 U.S.C. 7453, the 10% night differential covers every hour of a shift if at least four hours fall between 6 p.m. and 6 a.m. If fewer do, only the hours inside that window qualify.

Who typically gets them

You are most likely to see differentials where work runs around the clock: hospitals and care homes, factories, warehouses, security and emergency services. Federal law sets them for some US government workers, which gives you concrete, sourced reference points:

What about national data? In the Bureau of Labor Statistics' employer cost survey for June 2026, shift differentials averaged $0.10 per hour worked across all civilian workers, or 0.2% of total compensation. That average includes the many workers who never earn a differential, so it says little about what a night worker actually receives.

How differentials affect US overtime

This is where paychecks most often go wrong. Under the FLSA, overtime is time and a half of your regular rate, and the regular rate includes all pay for employment apart from specific exclusions (DOL Fact Sheet #56A). Federal regulations at 29 CFR 778.207 specifically require night-shift differentials to be included, whether they are a percentage of the base rate or a set amount per hour.

When you earn more than one rate in a week, your regular rate is a weighted average: total earnings divided by total hours worked (29 CFR 778.115). The steps:

  1. Add up base pay for all hours, plus all differential pay for the week.
  2. Divide by total hours worked. That is your regular rate.
  3. Multiply the regular rate by 0.5, then by your overtime hours. That is the overtime premium.
  4. Add the premium to the straight-time total.

Example: 48 hours with 24 night hours

Your base is $20.00 and night hours pay $3.00 more. This week you work 48 hours, 24 of them on nights. Straight time: 48 × $20 = $960, plus 24 × $3 = $72, for $1,032. Regular rate: $1,032 ÷ 48 = $21.50. Overtime premium: 8 × $21.50 × 0.5 = $86. Total: $1,118.

If payroll instead pays overtime at 1.5 × the $20 base, you get 40 × $20 + 8 × $30 + $72 = $1,112. That is $6 short this week and $312 short over 52 similar weeks.

Contracts and union agreements can be more generous than this. If your employer uses a different method, ask in writing how your overtime rate is calculated. State laws can add their own rules too, which the overtime laws guide covers.

More worked examples

Example: whole shift vs window only

You work 7 p.m. to 7 a.m. and the night differential is $3.00 an hour. If only the hours between 11 p.m. and 7 a.m. qualify, you earn 8 × $3 = $24 per shift. If the whole shift qualifies, you earn 12 × $3 = $36. At three shifts a week, that is a $36 weekly gap, or $1,872 over 52 weeks.

Example: flat vs percentage over a year

You expect about 1,000 night hours this year at a $30.00 base. A $2.00 flat differential pays $2,000, while a 10% differential pays $3,000. At an $18.00 base, the same 10% pays $1,800, so the flat $2.00 would be worth more.

Example: stacked premiums

Your employer pays $2.50 extra for night hours and $1.50 extra for weekend hours, and the written policy says they stack. A 10-hour Saturday night shift at a $22.00 base pays 10 × ($22.00 + $2.50 + $1.50) = $260, versus $220 at base rate alone.

How to check your pay stub

  1. Get the policy in writing: the window, the amount, whether the whole shift qualifies, and whether weekend or holiday premiums stack.
  2. Count your qualifying hours from your own log or schedule. The time card calculator totals each shift for you.
  3. Match the line items. Qualifying hours × differential should equal the differential line on your stub.
  4. Check the overtime rate in any week with both differentials and overtime. If it is exactly 1.5 × your base rate, ask how the regular rate was calculated.
  5. Watch the edges: shifts that start before or end after the window, holidays, training hours and call-backs.
  6. Keep records: save schedules, pay stubs and your own hours log.
Tip: Run your hours and rates through the 824824 shift differential calculator, then compare the result with your stub line by line.

Negotiating a differential

If you are weighing a job offer or a move to nights, treat the differential as part of the total package:

  • Ask for the exact policy, not just the headline number, including the qualifying window and how partial shifts are handled.
  • Prefer a percentage if you expect raises, or negotiate a flat amount that is reviewed with each pay increase.
  • Ask whether premiums stack, for example night plus weekend, and whether they apply to training and call-back hours.
  • Use real reference points, such as the federal rates above or a published union contract in your field, rather than unsourced "average" figures.
  • Get changes confirmed in writing before your first differential shift, so your first pay stub can be checked against something concrete.

Questions people ask

Is shift differential pay required by law in the US?

Generally no. The FLSA does not require extra pay for night work, so differentials come from employer policy, a union contract or, for some public employees, a statute.

Does a shift differential count toward overtime pay?

Under the FLSA, yes. Night-shift differentials must be included in the regular rate used to calculate overtime, whether they are paid as a percentage of the base rate or as a set amount per hour.

Is a flat or a percentage differential better?

Divide the flat amount by the percentage to find the break-even base rate. Above that rate the percentage pays more, and a percentage also rises automatically with every raise.

Do UK employers have to pay more for night shifts?

No. The National Minimum Wage applies to night workers, but there is no higher legal night rate. Any night premium comes from your contract.

How do penalty rates work in Australia?

Penalty rates are higher pay rates for particular hours or days, such as late-night shifts, weekends and public holidays. The rates that apply depend on the award or registered agreement that covers you.
