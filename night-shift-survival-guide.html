Night Shift Survival Guide

Updated October 8, 20268 min readBy the 824824 editorial team

On this page

  1. Plan your sleep around the shift
  2. Make daytime sleep possible
  3. Use light to your advantage
  4. Time your caffeine
  5. Eat and drink for nights
  6. Get home safely
  7. Days off and social life

Working nights means sleeping while the world is awake and staying sharp when your body expects to sleep. The strategies below come from the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), the American Academy of Sleep Medicine and other sleep authorities. They'll help you protect your sleep, stay alert on shift and get home safely.

Important: This guide is general wellness information, not medical advice. If you have ongoing trouble sleeping, feel very sleepy at work, or have nodded off while driving, talk to a doctor or another qualified clinician.

Plan your sleep around the shift

Night work runs against your internal body clock, which expects you to be awake by day and asleep at night. You can't switch it off, but you can give it as much consistency as possible.

After a night shift

NIOSH advises going to sleep as soon as you get home and sleeping as long as you can. If one long sleep doesn't work for you, the Sleep Foundation describes a split approach: sleep a few hours after your shift, then take a longer nap that ends close to your next shift.

Keep an anchor

Anchor sleep is a core block of sleep you keep at the same time every day, workdays and days off alike, so your body clock gets some consistency even when the rest of your schedule moves.

Example: anchor sleep on a 7 p.m. to 7 a.m. schedule

On workdays you're asleep from 8 a.m. to 3 p.m. On days off, NIOSH suggests staying up until 3 or 4 a.m. and sleeping until noon or 1 p.m.; say 4 a.m. to noon. Both schedules include 8 a.m. to noon, so that window is sleep time every day of the week.

Starting and ending a run of nights

Don't stay awake all day before your first night shift or all day after your last one; NIOSH warns that too many hours awake raise the risk of accidents. A 20 to 30 minute nap before a shift or during a break can also improve alertness, according to the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM). If your schedule rotates, the AASM suggests asking for a forward rotation (days, then evenings, then nights), because staying up later is easier than going to bed earlier. Our rotating shift patterns guide compares common schedules, and the night shift sleep planner can help you sketch a sleep schedule around your shifts.

Make daytime sleep possible

Your bedroom has to fight daylight, traffic and a household that's awake. The Sleep Foundation's shift work tips cover the basics:

  • Dark: blackout curtains or an eye mask.
  • Quiet: earplugs or a white noise machine.
  • Cool: the Sleep Foundation puts the ideal bedroom temperature around 65°F.
  • Protected: ask family or roommates not to disturb you during your sleep hours.
  • Wind-down: avoid heavy meals, alcohol and caffeine in the hours before you sleep.
Tip: Put your sleep hours on the shared family calendar so appointments, deliveries and visitors don't land in the middle of them.

Use light to your advantage

Light tells your body clock whether it's day or night, so when you get it matters. NIOSH's guidance for night-shift nurses breaks it down by part of the shift:

  • First half of the shift: get as much bright light as you can. Working in a brightly lit area is enough; you don't need a special lamp.
  • Second half: where it's safe, cut back on bright light so sleep comes more easily later. If you're very sleepy, though, bright light can help keep you alert.
  • The trip home: morning sun makes daytime sleep harder, and the AASM suggests avoiding it. Sunglasses help, but NIOSH cautions they can also block light's alerting effect and make drowsy driving more likely. Wear them only when someone else is driving.

For persistent problems, clinicians sometimes use bright light therapy timed to your shift. Talk to one before buying a light box.

Time your caffeine

Caffeine helps on a night shift, but it lingers. Its half-life, the time your body takes to clear half of it, averages about 5 hours in healthy people and ranges from roughly 1.5 to 9.5 hours, according to an Institute of Medicine report. Smoking speeds clearance up, while oral contraceptives can double the half-life.

You can also lose sleep without feeling it. In a small study in the Journal of Clinical Sleep Medicine, 400 mg of caffeine taken 6 hours before bedtime cut measured sleep by more than an hour, yet participants didn't seem to notice.

Example: the 4 a.m. coffee

Your shift ends at 7 a.m. and you plan to sleep at 8 a.m. A drink with 200 mg of caffeine at 4 a.m. leaves about 115 mg in your system at bedtime if your half-life is 5 hours. The same drink at midnight leaves about 66 mg. If you clear caffeine slowly, more is left either way.

  • Use caffeine in moderation and stop in the later part of the shift, as the AASM recommends.
  • The Sleep Foundation suggests small, spaced-out amounts rather than one big dose, which can lead to a crash.
  • Caffeine can't replace sleep. Needing more and more of it is a sign to look at your sleep.

Eat and drink for nights

NIOSH's diet suggestions for night workers come down to keeping a fairly normal rhythm: three meals per 24 hours, and cutting back on or avoiding food between midnight and 6 a.m. In practice, that rhythm can look like this:

  • Before your shift: eat a proper meal around your usual dinnertime.
  • During the shift: eat before midnight if you can. If you need fuel later, choose vegetables, salad, soup, fruit, whole-grain sandwiches, yogurt, eggs or nuts.
  • Go easy on sugar: sugary foods and low-fiber carbohydrates can make you sleepier when you need to stay alert.
  • After the shift: if you're hungry, keep it small, and skip heavy meals and alcohol before sleep.
  • Water: keep a bottle at your station and choose it over sugary drinks. If bathroom trips wake you, drink more early in the shift and less near the end.

Get home safely

The drive home can be the riskiest part of a night shift. NIOSH notes that most drowsy-driving crashes and near misses happen between 4 and 6 a.m., with other peaks from midnight to 2 a.m. and 2 to 4 p.m. In a 2011 American Nurses Association survey that NIOSH cites, 1 in 10 nurses reported a car crash they believed was linked to shift-work fatigue.

Know the warning signs:

  • Heavy eyelids, frequent blinking or trouble focusing
  • Repeated yawning or rubbing your eyes
  • Wandering, disconnected thoughts
  • No memory of the last few miles, or missed exits and signs
  • Drifting from your lane, tailgating or hitting a rumble strip

If you're struggling to stay awake, you're already impaired, so pull over somewhere safe. Rolling down the window or turning up the radio isn't supported by research, and short trips aren't automatically safe: in a survey NIOSH cites, almost 60% of people who fell asleep at the wheel had been driving for less than an hour.

Tip: Set up a backup plan before your run of nights starts: a ride from family, a coworker carpool, transit or a taxi, or a nap at work before you leave if that's allowed. If you're badly sleep-deprived, NIOSH cautions that caffeine and a short nap may not be enough to make driving safe.

Days off and social life

The Sleep Foundation suggests keeping bed and wake times consistent on days off, even though that can cost you some social time. A late schedule that holds on to your anchor sleep is a workable compromise.

  • Use the late schedule: staying up until 3 or 4 a.m. and sleeping until noon or 1 p.m. leaves afternoons and evenings free for family and friends.
  • Shift gradually: before a big schedule change, such as moving to day shifts, the AASM suggests moving your sleep time a little at a time over several days.
  • Share your schedule: give the people close to you your shift calendar and sleep hours, and plan regular time together, like dinner before a shift or a late breakfast on a day off.
  • Protect sleep first: book appointments and errands outside your sleep window whenever you can.

Know the signs of shift work disorder

Shift work disorder is a recognized circadian rhythm sleep disorder that develops in some, but not all, shift workers when work hours conflict with the body clock. The AASM lists these common signs:

  • Excessive sleepiness when you need to be awake
  • Trouble falling or staying asleep when you try to sleep
  • Low energy and difficulty concentrating
  • Headaches
  • Low mood and irritability

Talk to a clinician if these problems persist despite good sleep habits; the Sleep Foundation suggests seeing a doctor if symptoms last two weeks or longer. Go sooner if you've nodded off at work or behind the wheel. A clinician may ask you to keep a sleep diary for two weeks and review it alongside your work schedule. According to the AASM, a sleep study is usually ordered only if another condition, such as sleep apnea, is suspected. Treatment can include timed bright light, short-term sleep medication or melatonin, used under a clinician's guidance.

Questions people ask

What is anchor sleep?

Anchor sleep is a core block of sleep you keep at the same time every day, on workdays and days off. For example, sleeping 8 a.m. to 3 p.m. after night shifts and 4 a.m. to noon on days off keeps 8 a.m. to noon as sleep time every day.

When should I stop drinking caffeine on a night shift?

Caffeine's half-life averages about 5 hours and can be much longer, and in one study a large dose taken 6 hours before bed cut sleep by more than an hour. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine advises using it in moderation and stopping in the later part of your shift.

Should I wear sunglasses on the drive home after a night shift?

Blocking morning light can make it easier to fall asleep once you're home. NIOSH cautions that sunglasses also block light's alerting effect, so wear them only when someone else is driving.

How long should a nap before a night shift be?

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine suggests a 20 to 30 minute nap before a night shift or on a break. Before the first night shift in a run, also avoid staying awake all day.

What are the signs of shift work disorder?

Common signs include trouble sleeping when you try to sleep, heavy sleepiness at work, low energy, poor concentration, headaches and irritability. If problems persist for a few weeks or affect your work, safety or home life, talk to a clinician.

Is it safe to drive home after a night shift?

It can be risky, because NIOSH notes that most drowsy-driving crashes and near misses happen between 4 and 6 a.m. If you notice warning signs such as heavy eyelids or drifting from your lane, stop driving and arrange another way home next time.
